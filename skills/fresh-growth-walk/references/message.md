## The One Question

> Which stage of awareness is this reader in — and is this copy written for
> that stage?

---

## The six masters

### 1. Eugene Schwartz — *the five stages of awareness*

The spine of this group, and the rule most likely to produce the largest
finding in any run.

A reader arrives at one of five levels, and the headline's only job is to
meet them there:

| Stage | What they know | What the headline must do |
|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | Nothing — no problem named | Name the problem in their language |
| **Problem-aware** | They have the pain, not the solution | Show a solution category exists |
| **Solution-aware** | They know the category | Show why this one |
| **Product-aware** | They know you | Give the reason to act now |
| **Most-aware** | Ready | The offer, plainly |

His second idea, **market sophistication**, is that a market hardens over
time: the direct claim that worked on a fresh market gets tuned out on a
tired one, and copy must escalate to mechanism, then to identity.

**Where this bites hardest** is a product creating a category rather than
competing in one. That places most arrivals at *unaware* or *problem-aware* —
they have the pain but no name for the category that solves it. Copy opening
with the category name is speaking solution-aware language to a
problem-aware reader, and the reader experiences that as *"I don't know what
this is."*

**State this as a hypothesis the walk tests, never as a finding asserted in
advance** (§0.1). The evidence is §2.5: eight seconds on the page, then write
down what you believe it is.

> **In a walk:** for each door, name the stage it delivers. Organic search
> and a package-registry listing deliver very different readers to the same
> page.

### 2. April Dunford — *positioning is a competitive act*

Her method: **competitive alternatives → unique attributes → value → who
cares most → market category.** Positioning is not a description of what you
are; it is a claim about what you are *instead of*.

The load-bearing step is the first. If you cannot name what the reader would
do otherwise, the positioning is decoration (§2.2).

Her warning about category is direct: **choosing a category you must teach is
choosing to spend your budget teaching it.** A legitimate strategy and an
expensive one — it should be chosen knowingly rather than drifted into.

The honest competitive alternative is often not another tool. For
decision-support products it is usually *a confident person with an opinion*
— free, fast, already trusted, requiring no onboarding.

> **In a walk:** if the page never acknowledges what the reader does today,
> that is the §2.2 fail. Quote the absence.

### 3. David Ogilvy — *specifics, and the promise*

The headline carries a promise, and **specifics outsell adjectives**. His
canonical example — the loudest noise at sixty miles an hour coming from the
electric clock — contains no adjective at all.

The walkable test (§2.4): **delete every adjective and see whether the
meaning changed.** Adjectives that survive deletion were doing no work, and
their count is the finding. "Powerful, intelligent, seamless" survives
deletion perfectly, which is exactly the problem.

> **In a walk:** quote the sentence before and after deletion. That is the
> evidence.

### 4. Claude Hopkins — *every claim carries its proof*

*Scientific Advertising*, 1923, and still the strictest standard here: a
claim is worth nothing without its specific evidence attached, **in reach of
the claim** (§2.3). Not on another page. Not one click away.

Its virtue is that it is **testable**: for each claim on a page, either the
proof is adjacent or it is not.

> **In a walk:** list the page's claims in order and mark each
> proof-adjacent / proof-remote / unproved.

### 5. Joanna Wiebe — *the customer's words beat yours*

The best-performing lines are usually **harvested, not written** — pulled
from reviews, support tickets, sales calls, the customer's own phrasing. Copy
written from inside a company reliably uses the company's vocabulary for the
customer's problem.

It is sharpest where the product's own promise is about listening — a site
written entirely in internal vocabulary while selling something that claims
to surface the customer's is a coherence problem, not merely a copy one. The
general form: **where the copy contradicts the pitch, the copy is the
evidence.**

> **In a walk:** name where each headline's language came from. If it came
> from the product, that is the finding.

### 6. Robert Cialdini — *read as a detector*

Reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity. Here
these are used **only as a detector** (§2.7): find a technique deployed
without a matching truth behind it.

- Scarcity with no real limit → a defect.
- Social proof implying customers who do not exist → a defect, and a serious
  one.
- Authority borrowed from a logo the company has no relationship with → a
  defect.

The rule is never "add more persuasion". It is that a technique present
without its truth is **a claim without proof** (§2.3) wearing psychology's
clothes.

> **In a walk:** every technique found gets the question *"and is the
> underlying thing true?"* A no is severity 3 or higher.

## When there is no logo wall

A product early enough to be worth auditing this way usually has few named
customers and little volume. Every stranger walk will hit that, and the
finding *"there is no social proof"* is true, useless and unactionable.

The rule that replaces it is §2.6: **evidence is the social proof.** A
specific, checkable demonstration of the thing working does the job a logo
wall would do later — and does it for a reader who would discount the logos
anyway.

Report the absence of evidence, never the absence of logos.

## Applying it

1. Name the awareness stage each door delivers (Schwartz).
2. Check the page names a real competitive alternative (Dunford).
3. Delete the adjectives; quote before and after (Ogilvy).
4. Mark each claim proof-adjacent / proof-remote / unproved (Hopkins).
5. Name whose vocabulary each headline is in (Wiebe).
6. For every persuasion technique, ask whether the underlying thing is true
   (Cialdini).
7. Eight seconds, then write what you believe it is (§2.5).
